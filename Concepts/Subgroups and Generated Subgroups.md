# Subgroups and Generated Subgroups

For a [[Groups|group]] $G$, a subset $H\subseteq G$ is a **subgroup**, written $H\le G$, if:

- $e_G\in H$.
- $ab\in H$ for every $a,b\in H$.
- $a^{-1}\in H$ for every $a\in H$.

It is a group with the operation and identity inherited from $G$. Both $G$ and $I_G=\{e_G\}$ are subgroups; $I_G$ is the **trivial subgroup**.

**Source terminology:** the lecture calls subgroups other than both $G$ and $I_G$ “proper”. Standard usage requires only $H\ne G$, so it may include $I_G$.

Examples include
$$\mathbb Z\le\mathbb Q\le\mathbb R\le\mathbb C$$
under addition, $C_n\le C_m$ for $n\mid m$ among groups of [[Roots of Unity|roots of unity]], and the three rotations inside the triangle symmetry group.

## Subgroups of the additive integers

Proposition 1.16 states that all subgroups of $\mathbb Z$ are
$$n\mathbb Z=\{nk:k\in\mathbb Z\},\qquad n\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}.$$
The zero value gives $0\mathbb Z=\{0\}$.

Each $n\mathbb Z$ contains $0$ and is closed under addition and additive inverses:
$$nk+n\ell=n(k+\ell),\qquad -(nk)=n(-k).$$

Conversely, let $S\le\mathbb Z$ be non-trivial. It contains a positive integer, so let $n$ be its smallest positive integer. Closure under addition and inverses gives $n\mathbb Z\subseteq S$.

If $x\in S\setminus n\mathbb Z$, division with remainder gives
$$x=nq+r,\qquad q\in\mathbb Z,\quad 0<r<n.$$
Then $r=x-nq\in S$, contradicting the choice of $n$. Thus $S=n\mathbb Z$.

## Intersections and generation

For a family of subgroups $H_i\le G$ indexed by $i\in I$,
$$\bigcap_{i\in I}H_i
=\{a\in G:a\in H_i\text{ for every }i\in I\}\le G.$$
The two-subgroup case is referenced to question 2 of [[Groups - Example Sheet 1 2025]]; its exercise proof is not supplied in the lecture.

For $X\subseteq G$, Definition 1.17 sets
$$\langle X\rangle=\bigcap_{X\subseteq H\le G}H,$$
the **subgroup generated by $X$**, equivalently the smallest subgroup containing $X$. If $G=\langle X\rangle$, then $X$ **generates** $G$.

Lemma 1.18 states that **if $X$ generates $G$**, every $g\in G$ is a finite product
$$g=x_1^{\varepsilon_1}\cdots x_m^{\varepsilon_m},\qquad x_i\in X,\quad \varepsilon_i\in\{1,-1\},\quad m\ge0.$$
The empty product is $e_G$. Its proof is assigned as question 9 of the printed sheet; the lecture supplies no proof.

## A single generator

For $g\in G$, Definition 1.39 gives
$$\langle g\rangle=\{g^k:k\in\mathbb Z\}\le G.$$
It is cyclic. [[Cyclic Groups and Element Orders]] records the classification as $C_n$ or $\mathbb Z$ and the order $o(g)=|\langle g\rangle|$.

Source: [[Groups - Chapter 1 Examples and Definitions]], pages 16–18 and 32. Related: [[Bijections and Permutations]].

## Reading the dependencies — supplementary explanation

The least positive element in the integer-subgroup proof exists by [[Natural Numbers and Mathematical Induction|well-ordering]]. The division step uses [[Greatest Common Divisors and Euclid's Algorithm|division with remainder]]. Although that note states the theorem for positive dividends, it extends to any integer $x$: choose a sufficiently large integer $M$ with $x+Mn>0$, divide $x+Mn=qn+r$, then write $x=(q-M)n+r$.

The family in the definition of $\langle X\rangle$ is non-empty because it contains $G$. Its intersection contains $X$ and lies in every subgroup containing $X$, which explains “smallest”. For an arbitrary subset $X$, the finite-product description applies to elements of $\langle X\rangle$, not necessarily to all of $G$. For example, $\langle\{2\}\rangle=2\mathbb Z$ in the additive group $\mathbb Z$; it does not contain $1$. If $X=\varnothing$, only the empty product remains and $\langle\varnothing\rangle=\{e_G\}$.
