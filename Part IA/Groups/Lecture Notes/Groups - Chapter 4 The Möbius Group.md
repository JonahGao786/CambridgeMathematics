# Groups — Chapter 4: The Möbius Group

> [!abstract] Overview
> The Riemann sphere $\hat{\mathbb C}=\mathbb C\cup\{\infty\}$, pictured by stereographic projection. Möbius transformations $z\mapsto\frac{az+b}{cz+d}$ with $ad-bc\ne0$ compose like $2\times2$ matrices multiply, and they form a subgroup of $\operatorname{Sym}(\hat{\mathbb C})$: the Möbius group. A Möbius map other than the identity has at most two fixed points (three-point lemma), any three distinct points can be sent to any three, and every non-identity element is conjugate to a dilation or a translation. The group is generated by dilations, $z\mapsto z+1$ and inversion. Möbius maps send circles to circles, and preserve the cross-ratio, which detects when four points lie on a circle.
> Prerequisites: [[Groups - Chapter 1 Examples and Definitions]] (subgroups, $\operatorname{Sym}(X)$); [[Complex Numbers]].

## 4.1 The Riemann sphere

### Definition 4.1 — Riemann sphere

The **Riemann sphere** is
$$\hat{\mathbb C}=\mathbb C\cup\{\infty\}\qquad(\text{also written }\mathbb C_\infty),$$
where $\infty$ is a formal extra point "at infinity".

**Why is this a "sphere"? Stereographic projection.**

```tikz

  \node[blue] at (-1.75,-0.25) {$C$};
  % sphere
  \draw[thick] (0,0) circle (1);

  \draw[blue] (-1,0) arc (180:360:1 and 0.22);
  % north pole and projection line
  \fill (0,1) circle (1.2pt) node[above,red] {$N$};
  \draw[red,thick] (0,1) -- (1.9,-0.35);
  \fill (0.944,0.329) circle (1.2pt) node[above right] {$P$};
  \fill[red] (1.9,-0.35) circle (1.2pt) node[right,red] {$\pi(P)=z$};

```
*Stereographic projection: the line from the north pole $N$ through $P$ meets the plane $\mathbb C$ at $z=\pi(P)$.*

For $P\ne N$ on the unit sphere $S^2$, $z=\pi(P)\in\mathbb C$ is the unique intersection point of the line in $\mathbb R^3$ through $N$ and $P$ with $\mathbb C$. This gives a one-to-one correspondence between $S^2\setminus\{N\}$ and $\mathbb C$, and $N$ corresponds to $\infty\in\hat{\mathbb C}$.

## 4.2 Möbius transformations

### Definition 4.2 — Möbius transformation

Let $a,b,c,d\in\mathbb C$ with $ad-bc\ne0$. The corresponding **Möbius transformation** $\mu:\hat{\mathbb C}\to\hat{\mathbb C}$ is:

for $c\ne0$,
$$z\mapsto\begin{cases}\dfrac{az+b}{cz+d}&\text{if }z\in\mathbb C\setminus\{-\frac dc\},\\[2mm]\infty&\text{if }z=-\frac dc,\\[1mm]\dfrac ac&\text{if }z=\infty;\end{cases}$$
for $c=0$,
$$z\mapsto\begin{cases}\dfrac{az+b}d&\text{if }z\in\mathbb C,\\[2mm]\infty&\text{if }z=\infty.\end{cases}$$

**Notation.** We usually write $\mu(z)=\frac{az+b}{cz+d}$ for all cases.

**N.B.** $ad-bc=\det\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}$. We'll see: $ad-bc\ne0\Rightarrow\mu$ invertible.

### Composition of Möbius transformations

Say
$$\mu_1(z)=\frac{a_1z+b_1}{c_1z+d_1},\qquad\mu_2(z)=\frac{a_2z+b_2}{c_2z+d_2}.$$
**Exercise.**
$$\mu_1\circ\mu_2(z)=\frac{(a_1a_2+b_1c_2)z+(a_1b_2+b_1d_2)}{(c_1a_2+d_1c_2)z+(c_1b_2+d_1d_2)}.\tag{$*$}$$
Compare the formula for matrix multiplication:
$$\begin{pmatrix}a_1&b_1\\c_1&d_1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a_2&b_2\\c_2&d_2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}a_1a_2+b_1c_2&a_1b_2+b_1d_2\\c_1a_2+d_1c_2&c_1b_2+d_1d_2\end{pmatrix}.$$

> [!note] Explanation
> To get $(*)$, substitute $w=\mu_2(z)$ into $\mu_1(w)=\frac{a_1w+b_1}{c_1w+d_1}$ and multiply top and bottom by $c_2z+d_2$. The four coefficients that come out are exactly the entries of the matrix product, so composing Möbius transformations corresponds to multiplying their coefficient matrices.

### Theorem 4.3 — The Möbius group

Let
$$\mathcal M=\{f:\hat{\mathbb C}\to\hat{\mathbb C}:f\text{ is a Möbius transformation}\}.$$
Then $\mathcal M$ is a subgroup of $\operatorname{Sym}(\hat{\mathbb C})$, called the **Möbius group**.

**Proof.** *$\mathcal M\subseteq\operatorname{Sym}(\hat{\mathbb C})$?* Given $\mu:z\mapsto\frac{az+b}{cz+d}$ in $\mathcal M$, let
$$\nu:z\mapsto\frac{dz-b}{-cz+a},$$
also in $\mathcal M$ (its determinant is $da-bc\ne0$). By $(*)$,
$$\mu\circ\nu(z)=\frac{(ad-bc)z}{ad-bc}=z,\tag{$\dagger$}$$
using $ad-bc\ne0$. Similarly $\nu\circ\mu(z)=z$, so $\mu$ is a bijection: $\mu\in\operatorname{Sym}(\hat{\mathbb C})$.
- $\operatorname{id}_{\hat{\mathbb C}}\in\mathcal M$ (take $a=d=1$, $b=c=0$).
- $\mathcal M$ is closed under composition, by $(*)$ and the product formula for determinants:
$$\det\left[\begin{pmatrix}a_1&b_1\\c_1&d_1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a_2&b_2\\c_2&d_2\end{pmatrix}\right]=\det\begin{pmatrix}a_1&b_1\\c_1&d_1\end{pmatrix}\det\begin{pmatrix}a_2&b_2\\c_2&d_2\end{pmatrix}\ne0.$$
- $\mathcal M$ has inverses, by $(\dagger)$. $\square$

## 4.3 Fixed points

### Definition 4.4 — Fixed point

Suppose $f:X\to X$. Any $x\in X$ with $f(x)=x$ is called a **fixed point** of $f$. The set of fixed points is $\operatorname{Fix}(f)$.

**Examples** on $X=\hat{\mathbb C}$:
1. $f:z\mapsto z+1$ has $\operatorname{Fix}(f)=\{\infty\}$.
2. $f:z\mapsto2z$ has $\operatorname{Fix}(f)=\{0,\infty\}$.
3. Let $\mu:z\mapsto\frac{az+b}{cz+d}$ be a Möbius map. Observe:
   - (a) If $c\ne0$: $\infty\notin\operatorname{Fix}(\mu)$, and $w\in\mathbb C$ is in $\operatorname{Fix}(\mu)$ if and only if
$$w=\frac{aw+b}{cw+d}\iff cw^2+(d-a)w-b=0,$$
a quadratic with $c\ne0$, so $1$ or $2$ solutions.
   - (b) If $c=0$: $\infty\in\operatorname{Fix}(\mu)$, and $w\in\mathbb C$ is in $\operatorname{Fix}(\mu)$ if and only if $(d-a)w-b=0$: $0$, $1$ or infinitely many solutions.

In particular, every $\mu\in\mathcal M$ has at least one fixed point in $\hat{\mathbb C}$.

### Lemma 4.5 — Three-point lemma

If $\mu\in\mathcal M$ has $3$ different fixed points in $\hat{\mathbb C}$, then $\mu=\operatorname{id}_{\hat{\mathbb C}}$.

**Proof.** Say $\mu(z)=\frac{az+b}{cz+d}$ with $\operatorname{Fix}(\mu)\supseteq\{w_1,w_2,w_3\}$ (all different).

*Case 1: $\infty\notin\operatorname{Fix}(\mu)$.* By Example 3(a), the quadratic equation $cz^2+(d-a)z-b=0$ has $3$ distinct roots. So all its coefficients vanish; in particular $c=0$, contradicting $\infty\notin\operatorname{Fix}(\mu)$.

*Case 2: $\infty\in\operatorname{Fix}(\mu)$,* say $w_1=\infty$ after relabelling. Then $c=0$, and by Example 3(b) the linear equation $(d-a)z-b=0$ has (at least) two distinct roots $w_2,w_3$ in $\mathbb C$. So $d=a$, $b=0$, and $\mu=\operatorname{id}_{\hat{\mathbb C}}$. $\square$

### Lemma 4.6 — Three points to three points

For any triples of distinct points $z_1,z_2,z_3\in\hat{\mathbb C}$ and $w_1,w_2,w_3\in\hat{\mathbb C}$, there exists $\mu\in\mathcal M$ such that
$$\mu(z_i)=w_i,\qquad i=1,2,3.$$

**Remark.** By Lemma 4.5, $\mu$ is unique: if $\nu$ had the same property, then $\mu^{-1}\circ\nu$ would fix $3$ points, so $\mu^{-1}\circ\nu=\operatorname{id}_{\hat{\mathbb C}}$.

**Proof.** Set
$$\alpha:z\mapsto\frac{(z-z_1)(z_2-z_3)}{(z-z_3)(z_2-z_1)},$$
which is in $\mathcal M$ as the $z_i$ are distinct. Then
$$\alpha(z_1)=0,\qquad\alpha(z_2)=1,\qquad\alpha(z_3)=\infty.$$
Similarly
$$\beta:z\mapsto\frac{(z-w_1)(w_2-w_3)}{(z-w_3)(w_2-w_1)}$$
has $\beta(w_1)=0$, $\beta(w_2)=1$, $\beta(w_3)=\infty$. So $\mu=\beta^{-1}\circ\alpha$ works. $\square$

> [!note] Explanation
> $\alpha$ is built to send the three given points to the three "standard" points $0,1,\infty$: the factor $z-z_1$ makes $z_1\mapsto0$, the factor $z-z_3$ in the denominator makes $z_3\mapsto\infty$, and the constant $\frac{z_2-z_3}{z_2-z_1}$ is chosen so that $z_2\mapsto1$. Then $\beta^{-1}\circ\alpha$ goes $z_i\mapsto$ (standard point) $\mapsto w_i$.

**Observe.** For $g\in\mathcal M$,
$$f(z)=z\iff g\circ f\circ g^{-1}\big(g(z)\big)=g(z),$$
i.e. $\operatorname{Fix}(g\circ f\circ g^{-1})=g\big(\operatorname{Fix}(f)\big)$ (cf. [[Groups - Chapter 3 Group Actions#Definition 3.10 — Conjugacy classes|Slogan #2]]).

### Corollary 4.7 — Conjugate to a dilation or a translation

Suppose $f\in\mathcal M\setminus\{\operatorname{id}_{\hat{\mathbb C}}\}$. Then either
1. $|\operatorname{Fix}(f)|=2$ and $f$ is conjugate to $z\mapsto az$ for some $a\in\mathbb C\setminus\{0,1\}$ (a **dilation**), or
2. $|\operatorname{Fix}(f)|=1$ and $f$ is conjugate to $z\mapsto z+1$ (a **translation**).

**Proof.** (i) Say $\operatorname{Fix}(f)=\{w_1,w_2\}$. Pick $g\in\mathcal M$ with $g(w_1)=0$, $g(w_2)=\infty$ (Lemma 4.6), and let $F=g\circ f\circ g^{-1}$. Then $\operatorname{Fix}(F)=\{\infty,0\}$.
- As $\infty\in\operatorname{Fix}(F)$, we have $F(z)=az+b$ for some $a\ne0$.
- $F(0)=0$, so $b=0$.
- As $F$ has no other fixed points, $a\ne1$, and $F(z)=az$ with $a\in\mathbb C\setminus\{0,1\}$.

(ii) Say $\operatorname{Fix}(f)=\{w_1\}$. Pick $g\in\mathcal M$ with $g(w_1)=\infty$, and let $F=g\circ f\circ g^{-1}$, so $\operatorname{Fix}(F)=\{\infty\}$.
- As $\infty\in\operatorname{Fix}(F)$, we have $F(z)=az+b$ with $a\ne0$.
- Now $az+b=z$ has no roots in $\mathbb C$, so $a=1$ and $F:z\mapsto z+b$ for some $b\ne0$.

Now conjugate with $z\mapsto z/b$. $\square$

> [!note] Explanation
> By Definition 4.4, $f\ne\operatorname{id}$ has $1$ or $2$ fixed points (at least one, and at most two by Lemma 4.5), so the two cases cover everything. For the last step, with $h(z)=z/b$ we get $h\circ F\circ h^{-1}(z)=\frac{bz+b}b=z+1$.

**Remark.** In the proof above, $F^n=g\circ f^n\circ g^{-1}$ for all $n\in\mathbb N$. This is good for studying behaviour under iteration.

### Proposition 4.8 — Generators of the Möbius group

$\mathcal M$ is generated by elements of the following form:
1. $\mu_a:z\mapsto az$, $a\ne0$;
2. $\tau:z\mapsto z+1$;
3. $i:z\mapsto1/z$ (**inversion**).

**Proof.** Note $\mu_b\circ\tau\circ\mu_b^{-1}:z\mapsto z+b$, so all translations are generated.

Fix $\mu\in\mathcal M$ unknown, and say
$$\mu(0)=z_1,\qquad\mu(1)=z_2,\qquad\mu(\infty)=z_3.$$

*Step 1.* Construct $\mu_1$ with $\mu_1(z_3)=\infty$:
- if $z_3=\infty$, set $\mu_1=\operatorname{id}_{\hat{\mathbb C}}$;
- if $z_3\ne\infty$, set $\mu_1:z\mapsto z-z_3\mapsto\frac1{z-z_3}$ (a translation followed by inversion).

Let $z_1'=\mu_1(z_1)$, $z_2'=\mu_1(z_2)$.

*Step 2.* $\mu_2:z\mapsto z-z_1'$. We have
$$\mu_2\circ\mu_1(z_1)=0,\qquad\mu_2\circ\mu_1(z_3)=\mu_2(\infty)=\infty,\qquad\mu_2\circ\mu_1(z_2)=z_2''\ \text{say}.$$

*Step 3.* $\mu_3:z\mapsto z/z_2''$. Then
$$\mu_3\circ\mu_2\circ\mu_1(z_1)=\mu_3(0)=0,\qquad\mu_3\circ\mu_2\circ\mu_1(z_2)=1,\qquad\mu_3\circ\mu_2\circ\mu_1(z_3)=\infty.$$

By the three-point lemma, $\mu_3\circ\mu_2\circ\mu_1\circ\mu$ fixes $0$, $1$ and $\infty$, so it is $\operatorname{id}_{\hat{\mathbb C}}$. So
$$\mu=\mu_1^{-1}\circ\mu_2^{-1}\circ\mu_3^{-1}.\qquad\square$$

> [!note] Explanation
> Each $\mu_k$ is a composite of the three kinds of generator: $\mu_1$ is a translation then $i$, $\mu_2$ is a translation, and $\mu_3=\mu_{1/z_2''}$ (here $z_2''\ne0,\infty$ because $\mu_2\circ\mu_1$ is a bijection and already sends $z_1\mapsto0$, $z_3\mapsto\infty$). The inverses of generators are again of the same kinds ($\mu_a^{-1}=\mu_{1/a}$, $\tau^{-1}$ is translation by $-1$, $i^{-1}=i$), so $\mu$ is a product of generators.

## 4.4 Circles and Möbius transformations

### Definition 4.9 — Circle in the Riemann sphere

A **circle** in $\hat{\mathbb C}$ is:
1. either a Euclidean circle in $\mathbb C$, $\{z:|z-c|=r\}$;
2. or $\ell\cup\{\infty\}$ with $\ell\subseteq\mathbb C$ a Euclidean straight line. Every line is the set of points equidistant from two points $a,b$: $\{z:|z-a|=|z-b|\}$ (and this is fine for $\infty$ also).

*A circle $|z-c|=r$, and a line: the perpendicular bisector of $a$ and $b$.*

### Theorem 4.10 — Möbius maps send circles to circles

For any circle $C\subseteq\hat{\mathbb C}$ and $\mu\in\mathcal M$, $\mu(C)$ is also a circle (in the sense of Definition 4.9).

**Proof.** It is enough to check the claim for the generators of $\mathcal M$ (Proposition 4.8):
1. $\mu:z\mapsto kz$ ($k\ne0$) and
2. $\mu:z\mapsto z+1$ are easy checks, e.g. for (1), $|z-c|=r\iff|\mu(z)-\mu(c)|=|kz-kc|=|k|r$.
3. $\mu:z\mapsto\frac1z$.

*(a) $C$ is $|z-c|=r$.* Its image under $\mu$ is
$$\Big|\frac1z-c\Big|=r\iff\Big|\frac{1-cz}z\Big|=r.$$
- *Case 1: $|c|=r$ ($r\ne0$),* i.e. the circle passes through the origin. Rearranging gives
$$\Big|\frac1c-z\Big|=|z|,$$
a line.
- *Case 2: $|c|\ne r$,* i.e. the circle does not pass through the origin. Rearranging gives
$$\Big|z-\frac{\bar c}{|c|^2-r^2}\Big|=\frac r{\big||c|^2-r^2\big|},$$
a circle.

*(b) $C$ is a Euclidean line.* A similar manipulation (exercise). $\square$

> [!note] Explanation
> Case 1: $|1-cz|=r|z|=|c||z|$; divide by $|c|$ to get $|\frac1c-z|=|z|$, the perpendicular bisector of $0$ and $\frac1c$. Case 2: square $|1-cz|^2=r^2|z|^2$ to get $(|c|^2-r^2)|z|^2-2\operatorname{Re}(cz)+1=0$, divide by $|c|^2-r^2$ and complete the square; the centre is $\frac{\bar c}{|c|^2-r^2}$. The origin matters because $\frac1z$ sends $0$ to $\infty$: a circle through $0$ becomes a "circle through $\infty$", i.e. a line.

### Definition 4.11 — Cross-ratio

Let $z_1,z_2,z_3,z_4\in\hat{\mathbb C}$ be distinct, and let $\mu$ be the unique element of $\mathcal M$ with $\mu(z_1)=0$, $\mu(z_2)=1$, $\mu(z_3)=\infty$ (Lemma 4.6). The **cross-ratio** is
$$[z_1,z_2,z_3,z_4]=\mu(z_4)=\frac{(z_4-z_1)(z_2-z_3)}{(z_4-z_3)(z_2-z_1)},$$
the formula coming from the proof of Lemma 4.6.

> [!note] Explanation
> The name: it is a ratio of two ratios, $[z_1,z_2,z_3,z_4]=\dfrac{(z_4-z_1)/(z_4-z_3)}{(z_2-z_1)/(z_2-z_3)}$, comparing how $z_4$ and $z_2$ sit relative to $z_1$ and $z_3$.

### Proposition 4.12 — Cross-ratio is invariant

If $z_1,z_2,z_3,z_4\in\hat{\mathbb C}$ (distinct) and $g\in\mathcal M$, then
$$[z_1,z_2,z_3,z_4]=[g(z_1),g(z_2),g(z_3),g(z_4)].$$

**Proof.** Let $f\in\mathcal M$ be the unique map with $f(z_1)=0$, $f(z_2)=1$, $f(z_3)=\infty$, and let $F=f\circ g^{-1}$. Then
$$F(g(z_1))=f\circ g^{-1}(g(z_1))=f(z_1)=0,\qquad F(g(z_2))=1,\qquad F(g(z_3))=\infty.$$
So
$$\begin{aligned}
[g(z_1),g(z_2),g(z_3),g(z_4)]&=F(g(z_4)) &&(\text{definition, with }F)\\
&=f\circ g^{-1}(g(z_4))=f(z_4)\\
&=[z_1,z_2,z_3,z_4].
\end{aligned}$$
$\square$

### Corollary 4.13 — Four points on a circle

Four points $z_1,z_2,z_3,z_4\in\hat{\mathbb C}$ lie on a circle if and only if $[z_1,z_2,z_3,z_4]\in\mathbb R\cup\{\infty\}$.

**Proof.** Let $g\in\mathcal M$ with $z_1\mapsto0$, $z_2\mapsto1$ and $z_3\mapsto\infty$. Observe that $0,1,\infty$ all lie on the circle $\mathbb R\cup\{\infty\}$. So $z_1,z_2,z_3$ all lie on the circle $C=g^{-1}(\mathbb R\cup\{\infty\})$ (Theorem 4.10), which is the only circle through them. Now
$$z_4\in C\iff g(z_4)\in\mathbb R\cup\{\infty\}\iff[z_1,z_2,z_3,z_4]\in\mathbb R\cup\{\infty\},$$
the last step by definition of the cross-ratio. $\square$
